Before this semester, I thought I already knew how to think logically. I had done well in mathematics in school, I had picked up a little bit of coding here and there out of curiosity, and I walked into the first Programming Fundamentals lecture with a quiet confidence that I now look back on with some amusement. It took only a couple of weeks for that confidence to be replaced by something more useful: a genuine respect for how differently a computer needs you to think compared to how you think in everyday life.

Programming Fundamentals, or PF as everyone in my batch quickly started calling it, is one of the first real technical courses a Computer Engineering student takes. The course does not assume you know anything, and in some ways that is exactly what makes it so disorienting at first. You are asked to take a problem you could explain to a friend in one sentence — sort a list of numbers, find the largest value, check if a word is a palindrome — and break it down into a sequence of steps so precise and so unambiguous that a machine with no judgment of its own can execute them correctly every single time. There is no room for "you know what I mean" in programming. The computer does not know what you mean. It only knows what you wrote.

My early lectures were focused on the absolute basics: variables, data types, input and output, and the structure of a simple program from start to finish. It sounds almost too simple to be worth describing, but I remember the specific moment it stopped feeling simple — when Dr. Bilal Ahmad asked us to write a program that took three numbers and printed them in ascending order, without using any built-in sorting function. I had assumed this would take five minutes. It took me almost forty, because I kept writing logic that worked for some combinations of numbers and silently failed for others. That exercise, more than any lecture slide, taught me what it actually means for code to be correct. Correct does not mean it worked once. It means it works for every case you can think of, and ideally some you cannot.

What made the early weeks of PF particularly demanding was the sheer unfamiliarity of the mindset required. In everyday reasoning, we are used to skipping steps, relying on context, and letting ambiguity resolve itself naturally in conversation. Programming punishes all three of those habits. I found myself, during this period, developing a new kind of patience — the patience to write out every single step of a process on paper before typing a single line of code, because the moment I tried to skip ahead, I would lose track of some edge case that would later show up as a confusing bug.

I also started to notice, during these early weeks, how much of programming is really about communication — not with the computer exactly, but with my future self and with anyone else who might read the code later. A variable named `x` does the same job as a variable named `total_score`, but only one of them tells a story when you come back to the code a week later having forgotten your own reasoning. I did not fully appreciate this lesson at the time; it would take a later course in this same semester, focused entirely on communication, to make this connection explicit for me. But the seed of that idea was planted here, in these first weeks of PF, simply through the frustration of rereading my own messy code and not understanding what I had been thinking.

The lab sessions that accompanied the lectures were where the real learning happened, in my experience. Lectures gave me the vocabulary and the concepts, but it was only in the lab, staring at a program that refused to compile or that produced an output I could not explain, that the concepts actually became mine. I remember spending nearly an entire lab session chasing down a single misplaced semicolon, and the strange mixture of frustration and relief when I finally found it. There is a particular kind of humility that comes from being defeated by a single character, and I think every programmer needs to experience that humility early, because it teaches you to read your own code carefully rather than assuming you already know what it says.

Looking back at these first weeks now, from later in the semester, I can see how foundational this period actually was. Every later concept — loops, functions, data structures — depended on the basic discipline I was building here: thinking in precise, ordered steps, testing assumptions rather than trusting them, and accepting that being wrong is simply part of the process rather than a sign that you are bad at this. I did not understand any of that explicitly at the time. I just knew that the cup of tea beside me was going cold faster than I expected, and that somewhere in the gap between what I thought I had written and what the program actually did, there was a lesson waiting for me every single day.

This semester, as I am only now beginning to understand, was always going to be about more than memorizing syntax. It was about learning a new way of thinking that would eventually stretch across every other course I would take — including, as it would turn out only a couple of weeks later, an entirely different subject that would ask me to think in a completely different structure: not steps and sequences this time, but tables, relationships, and the rules that govern how real-world information should be organized. That subject was Database Systems, also taught by [Dr. Bilal Ahmad](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/), and the discipline I was building in these early PF weeks turned out to be exactly the kind of preparation I needed for it.
