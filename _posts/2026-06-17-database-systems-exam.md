Of all the exams I faced during this final exam week, the Database Systems exam on June 18th carried a special kind of weight for me. It was not just another subject on the schedule — it was the course taught by Dr. Bilal Ahmad, and everything we had built toward it over the semester made this exam feel like a culmination of months of genuine, hands-on learning. On the evening of June 17th, I settled in to revise, and I found myself doing something unusual: I was not just reviewing notes. I was reliving the journey of the entire semester in this course.

Database Systems, as Dr. Bilal Ahmad teaches it, is not merely an academic exercise in writing SQL queries. It is a subject grounded in the real-world problem of organizing, storing, and retrieving information in ways that are efficient, reliable, and meaningful. From the very first lecture, Dr. Ahmad had emphasized that a database is not just a table of data — it is a carefully designed structure that reflects the relationships and rules of the real world it represents. That philosophy shaped everything we did in the course, and it shaped my preparation on June 17th. I started my revision by going back to those early lectures, almost as a way of retracing the course's internal logic from the beginning, since I find that subjects taught with a coherent philosophy are best revised in the order that philosophy was originally built.

My revision covered the full spectrum of topics: the relational model, entity-relationship diagrams, SQL — including DDL and DML commands, joins, subqueries, and aggregate functions — and, crucially, normalization. Normalization was one of the core topics I spent significant time on that evening. Understanding why we normalize a database — to eliminate redundancy, ensure data integrity, and reduce anomalies — required not just memorizing the rules of 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF, but actually working through examples and understanding what each normal form achieves. First Normal Form ensures that each column contains atomic values. Second Normal Form eliminates partial dependencies. Third Normal Form goes further to eliminate transitive dependencies. Each level of normalization is a step toward a cleaner, more robust database design. I worked through several unnormalized tables by hand that evening, deliberately choosing examples I had not seen before, because I wanted to test whether I actually understood the underlying logic rather than simply recognizing patterns from familiar practice problems.

What made this revision particularly rich was the project our group had completed during the semester — a diabetes patient prediction system. This project was one of the most ambitious and rewarding things I worked on all year. Our group built a complete system that combined database management with machine learning. On the database side, we designed the schema, ensured proper normalization through 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF, wrote SQL queries to store and retrieve patient data, and structured the data in a way that could feed cleanly into a predictive model. On the machine learning side — and this is where the expertise of our professor as an AI, ML, and DL specialist truly shone — we trained a model on a diabetes dataset to predict patient outcomes. Going back through our own schema during revision was, in a strange way, more useful than any textbook example, because I already understood every design decision behind it and could explain, without hesitation, why we had chosen a particular key structure or why a certain table needed to be split during the normalization process.

Dr. Ahmad has a particular philosophy when it comes to choosing datasets for machine learning projects. He consistently pushes his students toward medical datasets, explaining that healthcare data is collected with a level of precision and accuracy that makes it ideal for training reliable models. The diabetes dataset we used was no exception — it was a well-structured, real-world dataset with clinical measurements and labeled outcomes. We used Python to build and train the model, incorporating libraries that handled the data processing and learning algorithms, and we built a user interface using Jinja2 and related tools where Dr. Ahmad could enter new patient data and receive predictions from the trained model. Watching the system generate predictions based on real data was one of the most exciting moments of the entire semester, and remembering that feeling on the night before the exam gave the revision a sense of purpose that pure memorization never quite achieves.

Going into the database exam on June 18th, I felt the benefit of having that project experience. The concepts on the exam — normalization, SQL, relational design — were not abstract for me. I had applied them to a real project, debugged real queries, and seen firsthand why a poorly designed database causes problems downstream. That practical experience translated into confidence in the exam room. When a question asked me to identify a functional dependency in an unfamiliar table, I found myself instinctively reaching for the same mental process I had used while designing our own patient schema months earlier, rather than trying to recall a rule from a slide.

The exam tested a range of skills, from writing correct SQL queries to designing normalized schemas from scratch. There were questions on joins, on identifying functional dependencies, on converting an unnormalized table through the normal forms step by step. For each question, I drew on both the theoretical framework I had revised and the hands-on intuition built through the project. I noticed, partway through the exam, that the questions which had initially looked the most intimidating on first read were often the ones I solved fastest, simply because they mapped so closely onto problems we had already solved for real during the project.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad's approach to this course had always balanced theory with application, and that balance was reflected in the exam itself. He did not ask us to simply recite definitions — he asked us to demonstrate understanding by applying concepts to real scenarios. That is the kind of teaching that sticks, the kind that builds engineers rather than exam-passers. Walking out of the database exam on June 18th, I felt something that went beyond relief at having finished. I felt genuinely proud of what I had learned and what I had built. This course had given me something durable — not just a grade, but a working understanding of how real systems that handle real data are designed, a skill I expect to draw on for the rest of my career in this field.
